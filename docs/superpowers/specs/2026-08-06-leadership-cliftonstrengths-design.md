# Article: Leadership, the first step, and what a failed leader owes (Design Spec)
**Date:** 2026-08-06
**Site:** tomatamagotato (blog post, standard post layout)

---

## 1. Purpose & Framing

A personal essay on what converts an ordinary person into a leader. Not a
definition of the leadership *role*, and not a management how-to. The subject is
the transition: the moment a person decides to move while everyone around them
is still unsure, and what that person then owes to the people who move because
they moved.

**Core positioning:** leadership is demonstration. The first step is taken alone
and is legible on purpose. People follow because they watched, not because they
were assigned. Everything downstream in the essay is a consequence of that one
claim.

**The step is toward something.** Courage under uncertainty is necessary and not
sufficient. A leader is executory. They build a plan, an idea of what the far
side looks like, and the first step is the first move in executing it. That is
what makes the step legible to the people watching, because they can see where it
goes. Movement alone is not leadership, it is just motion, and the essay must not
be readable as endorsing the inspiring person who is competent at nothing.

**The single test the essay proposes:** did the people around you leave more
capable than they arrived? This runs across success and failure without
splitting into cases. It is the essay's spine and its only real argument.

**Where the assessments sit:** late, subordinate, and doing two different jobs.
Neither produced the philosophy. The author held these views before taking
either. CliftonStrengths explains why *this author's* version of the first step
is unusually expensive. Social Styles appears only as self-reflection, as an
instrument the author answered inaccurately about himself, and is therefore
evidence for the introspection argument rather than a description of anyone.
Neither is ever the authority for the essay's claims.

**Audience:** the site's existing register. Peers. No selling of the concept, no
definitions of terms a reader already has. Rewards depth, punishes padding.

**Register:** first person, warm, argumentative. Assertions the author actually
holds, including the ones that grind against each other.

## 2. The Argument, Stated Plainly

Recorded here so the draft does not drift from it.

1. Everyone has the potential to lead. Potential is not the scarce thing. Not
   everyone becomes great at it, and greatness is not distributed by origin,
   which means nobody gets to rule themselves out in advance.
2. What converts potential into leadership is willingness to take the first step
   under uncertainty, knowing the cost may be embarrassment, pain, or suffering.
   The cost is real and it recurs, since leading is not one brave step.
3. A leader is executory. They build ideas into plans and plans into reality.
   The step is a step toward something already constructed, which is why it
   creates certainty for people who have nothing constructed of their own.
4. That step is visible. Visibility is the mechanism. People are inspired by
   what they watch someone do, not by instruction.
5. Therefore leadership does not require a title, a team, or an assignment. It
   requires someone who moves and someone who sees it.
6. Positional authority can compel compliance. It cannot compel belief. Trust is
   what converts compliance into following, and no rank grants it.
7. Because followers learn by watching, a leader is a teacher. Teaching here
   means demonstration, not instruction. What transfers is the method: how a
   person gets from nothing to a plan to a thing that exists.
8. Leaders expire. Every one of them. The measure of a leader is therefore what
   their people can do once the leader is gone. Because they learned the method
   rather than inheriting the plan, they can continue the work or start their
   own.
9. This gives one test that works on both the successful and the failed leader:
   did the people leave more capable?
10. Good intentions are a precondition for acting on others' behalf. They are not
    a defense once the damage is done. Nothing carries backward from the first to
    the second.
11. You cannot fully audit your own motives. This does not excuse you from
    trying. Most harm comes from people who never asked themselves what they
    were doing.
12. A leader who fails still owes the people who followed them. The debt is that
    the failure becomes something those people can grow from, rather than
    something that merely happened to them.
13. Sacrifice is required and it has a ceiling. Past some point it stops buying
    capability and starts destroying it, including the capability to lead at all,
    since a person with nothing left transmits the damage rather than the method.
    The essay shows this rather than stating it.

## 3. Structure

Section lengths must vary. Section 4 and Section 6 are the long ones. Section 3
is deliberately short.

**Load warning for Section 4.** It now carries the crisis leader, the
Machiavellian, Luthen, Cassian, and the philosophical positioning. That is more
than one section can hold at readable length. When drafting, either split it at
the Machiavellian boundary into two sections, or cut the crisis leader down to a
short opening move so the weight sits on the Machiavellian and Luthen pair, which
is where the argument actually is. Decide during drafting, and prefer the split
if the prose runs long. The essay must not have a section that reads as a
catalogue of examples.

### Section 1 — The first step

Opens on the claim that everyone has the potential and almost nobody spends it.
Establishes the cost: risk, embarrassment, pain, discomfort, and the fact that
the person stepping does not know whether A or B is correct. Certainty is not
available. Action is required anyway.

**The Ratatouille framing, used briefly.** Gusteau's "anyone can cook" is the
familiar half. The half that does the work is Ego's: not everyone can become a
great artist, but a great artist can come from anywhere. That is the essay's
claim about leadership. It refuses to promise the outcome and refuses to gatekeep
the attempt, which is more honest than "anyone can lead if they try hard enough"
and matches an essay that later argues the cost can destroy you.

Keep it to a few sentences. A reader who has not seen the film must lose nothing.
No plot, no character names beyond what the attribution needs, no summary.

**The builder claim belongs here, in this section, not later.** A leader is
executory. They build ideas into plans and plans into reality, builder in the
sense of construction rather than trade. The first step is not raw courage, it is
the first move in executing something already built in the head.

This is what supplies the certainty the section claims a leader creates. Do not
assert that certainty without explaining it. The leader is not certain they are
right. They have constructed a picture of the far side and committed to moving
toward it, and everyone else is uncertain because nobody has built anything to be
certain about. That is the whole difference.

Establishes early that leading does not require a group. A leader is a role
model before they are anything organizational.

Ends by naming the mechanism the rest of the essay depends on: the step is
watched. That is what makes it leadership rather than merely a decision.

### Section 2 — Why anyone follows

Trust. The counterargument arrives here rather than being avoided: what about
the officer who simply gives orders and is obeyed?

The answer is the essay's sharpest line and should be given room. Rank buys
compliance. It does not buy belief. *Why would I follow orders if I don't trust
you?* Compliance ends the moment the authority does. Following does not.

This section justifies the whole teaching frame that comes next, because nobody
learns from a person they do not trust. They only obey.

### Section 3 — A leader is a teacher

Short and load-bearing.

The argument from expiry: every leader runs out. Time, role, health, attention.
If the people around them can only wait for direction, the leader has built
nothing that survives contact with their own absence.

"They should be learning instead of watching" belongs here, or as close to here
as the prose allows. It is the essay's best sentence.

Define teaching explicitly as demonstration, not curriculum. This definition is
load-bearing for Section 4 and must be stated, not implied.

**What actually transfers is the method.** Demonstration on its own is thin, and
"be brave" is not a transferable skill. What people learn from watching a builder
is how a person goes from nothing to a plan to a thing that exists. This is why
the successors in the expiry argument are not limited to continuing the leader's
work. They did not inherit a plan. They watched someone build one, so they can go
build their own.

Growth and development are the *output* of leadership. Teaching is the mechanism
that produces it. Keep them distinct in the prose, since collapsing them makes
the section vague.

### Section 4 — Two leaders who look nothing alike

The essay's structural centerpiece and its longest section.

**The crisis leader.** Handled as the strongest case for the framework, not as
an objection to be survived. Someone in a burning building develops nobody.
They are still unambiguously leading. They act, they direct, they take people to
the exits, they coordinate the headcount. And what they demonstrate is that calm
was available under conditions where calm is not the default. Nobody in that
stairwell becomes a copy of them. It is still a learning moment. Teaching
stripped to demonstration alone, and it still holds.

**The Machiavellian.** The genuinely hard case, because the objective was
achieved. Judged on outcome, this leader passes. They meant well and they
delivered, and they rolled over their own people to do it.

The essay must state plainly that demonstration alone cannot be the test, since
this leader demonstrates too, and what their people learn is that this is what
winning requires. That lesson transfers, and it propagates. The direction has to
be attached to the test or the framework passes people it should not.

The sharpest formulation of the difference, and it should survive into the draft
in some form: this leader builds effectively. That is the whole difficulty of the
case. But they build *with* people rather than building people. Both are supposed
to happen at once. The plan gets built and so do the people executing it. The
Machiavellian achieves the first by spending the second.

So the test is not *did they teach*. It is *did the people leave more capable, or
more damaged*. The Machiavellian fails on the only measure that matters after
they are gone.

**The spymaster, and the ceiling on sacrifice.** Sits directly after the
Machiavellian, since the two are close relatives and the gap between them is
where the work happens. Both spend people. One books the cost against himself.

Luthen Rael from *Andor*. Used for one thing only: what he sacrificed. No ease,
no peace, no love, no sleep, no mirror to look into. He burns his decency for
someone else's future and his life for a sunrise he knows he will never see. He
would not read as a leader at first glance, no army and no title, and he is one.
He recruits, dispatches, and directs people who never see the whole plan.

The essay does not rescue him. He fails the test in Section 4 and the essay says
so plainly. He is Machiavellian, and morally he is atrocious, and he is still
leading.

**The ceiling is shown, never stated.** Do not write a principle like "sacrifice
has a limit." Show the ending instead: everyone left him. The one person who
stayed was raised into the work and carries the same emptiness he does, so she is
not the counterexample to his isolation, she is the evidence of what proximity to
him costs. He built an apprentice and the building is what damaged her. Let the
reader arrive at the conclusion that past some point the sacrifice stops buying
capability and starts destroying it.

This is the teaching test arriving from the other direction. What Luthen
transmits is the wound, and it propagates, and that is what disqualifies him
under his own results.

**Cassian as the contrast, in a clause or two, not a section.** Same currency,
different accounting. Luthen paid continuously until there was nothing left to
pay with. Cassian paid once, at the end, deliberately, for people he actually
knew, and he had become someone rather than hollowed himself out. State that
Cassian was a good soldier and a good man and not a good leader, which keeps him
from being installed as a hero of the framework. He is only proof that the
sacrifice can be made without the destruction being mandatory.

**Accuracy constraints for this passage.** Nobody came back for Luthen. Kleya
went back, and Cassian was pulled in after her, which is not the same thing and
the difference is the point. Do not write that Cassian returned for him. Avoid
any beat involving Cassian's father, the interrogation room, or Ferrix. The safe
version, and the only one needed, is that Cassian's arc ends in a sacrifice that
is chosen and finite.

**Length ceiling: two paragraphs.** The speech is quotable and the ending is
describable. Everything else is assumed or cut. This is not a review, and a
reader who has never seen the show must still follow the argument, which means
the argument cannot depend on knowing who any of these people are.

**Philosophical positioning.** The author rejects that the end justifies the
means and finds it cruel. The essay does not claim the utilitarian label,
because straight act-utilitarianism argues *for* the position being rejected and
a reader who knows the vocabulary will catch it. The position written is the
wider-accounting one: the Machiavellian did not actually maximize anything. They
used a small ledger. They booked the objective and expensed the people, and the
capability that never got built never enters their arithmetic. Count it and the
sum goes negative.

Keep this passage short and unlabeled. No philosophy-seminar vocabulary. The
argument stands without naming any school.

### Section 5 — Intentions

The section the author found hardest to resolve, and the essay does not fully
resolve it. That is deliberate and must not read as evasion, so the *shape* of
the problem is stated sharply even though the answer is not supplied.

Beats:

- The author's standing position: a person is judged by their actions, not their
  intentions.
- Why that grinds against the leadership frame, which asks people to act under
  uncertainty and therefore guarantees some of them will fail while meaning
  well.
- The distinction that resolves part of it: good intentions are the permission
  to act on someone else's behalf. Actions are the verdict. Nothing carries
  backward. Meaning well never buys a discount on the judgment, only the
  standing to have tried.
- Why that clean version is not enough. The examples where people genuinely
  meant well and destroyed something anyway: kids resent parents, parents resent
  kids, families come apart, relationships fail. Everyone involved had good
  intentions. It is what drove them to bad actions.
- The uncomfortable landing: the good-intentions defense is simultaneously the
  most human thing a person can offer and the most common cover story for harm,
  and you cannot tell which one you are looking at from the inside. Including
  when it is you.

### Section 6 — Introspection, and what a failed leader owes

The essay's emotional and argumentative floor. Long.

**Introspection first.** The limit from Section 5 is real but does not discharge
the obligation. You cannot audit your motives perfectly. That is not a licence to
skip the attempt. Most people never make the attempt at all, and the damage does
not come from evil, it comes from unexamined motion. People just *do*. The
essay's position: introspection is unreliable and mandatory, both.

**Then the debt.** A leader who fails still owes the people who followed them,
because those people were exposed by a decision that was not theirs. The
obligation survives the failure.

State what the debt consists of, because the specifics are the whole section:
staying rather than leaving, telling them plainly what went wrong instead of
managing the story, not letting them carry blame that belongs to the leader.

**Then the resolution of the whole essay.** The debt is not passive acceptance
of consequences, which is just something that happens to you. It is active. You
turn around and face the people who moved because you moved, and you convert the
failure into something they can grow from. Anyone can demonstrate well when it
is working. The obligation is to keep demonstrating when it is not, and what is
being demonstrated now is how to lose without lying about it.

This is where growth mindset lands, and it must be written as the harder version:
not resilience, not bouncing back, but the willingness to find out you were wrong
about yourself. Harder than being wrong about a decision, which is why most
people avoid it.

### Section 7 — CliftonStrengths, late

Enters only after the argument is complete. Framed honestly on arrival: the
assessment did not cause any of the above. The author held these views first.
The framework explains the mechanism, it did not supply the conclusion.

Top five, in order: **Deliberative, Responsibility, Activator, Analytical,
Context.**

The observation the section exists to make: four of these are brakes. Deliberative
slows down to assess risk. Analytical slows down to demand evidence. Context slows
down to find the origin story. Responsibility raises the cost of every commitment,
so fewer get made. Activator sits in the middle of that, alone, saying go.

What that produces is a person whose first step is expensive. Someone with
Command or Self-Assurance high takes the same step and pays almost nothing for
it. Same act, different internal price. From outside, the pause reads as
hesitation. It is not. It is cost accounting.

Tie back rather than trailing off: the brakes are asking *why am I about to do
this* before the Activator fires. The introspection argued for in Section 6 is
not a detour into self-help. It is the thing this profile does automatically, and
the essay is partly an argument that everyone else should do it deliberately.

Honest limits, briefly: the profile has costs. Deliberative reads as
disengagement to people who mistake speed for confidence. Responsibility paired
with Deliberative carries commitments past the point where a healthier person
would renegotiate.

**One more connection worth making.** Activator is normally described as
impatience with planning, action ending analysis. That appears to contradict the
builder claim in Section 1, where the plan precedes the step. It does not. The
plan gets built during the deliberation the four brakes force, so by the time the
Activator fires there is already a structure behind it. The pause that reads as
hesitation is not only cost accounting, it is construction.

**Explicitly not claimed:** that the absence of a Relationship Building theme in
the top five says anything about what the author values. The assessment measures
where instinct runs first, not what a person holds to be true. Relationship
building is non-negotiable to leadership in this essay's view. A leader brings
people together. That is stated positively somewhere in this section so the
omission cannot be misread.

### Section 7b — The other assessment, and getting it wrong

Follows directly from Section 7 and closes the essay's argument. Treated as one
movement with Section 7 in the prose, not a separate topic.

Two years before CliftonStrengths, the author took a Social Styles assessment and
scored as a **Driver**: high assertiveness, low responsiveness. The instrument
sorts on two axes into four boxes.

**State the instrument's weakness plainly and briefly.** A four-box model sorts
people by which side of an arbitrary midpoint they land on. Score just under and
just over the line and you are two different types. The post does not treat this
as equivalent evidence to anything, and saying so protects the section's
credibility. Keep it to a sentence or two, this is not a methodology essay.

**The contradiction is the point.** Driver requires scoring toward fast to
decide, taking risks, fast-paced, carefree, taking charge. Two of the items ask
about deliberation directly. Then CliftonStrengths returned Deliberative at rank
one, the most risk-averse and slowest-committing theme in the inventory, and
Analytical at four, which is the name of the opposite quadrant in Social Styles.
Both results cannot describe the same behavior.

**The author's own position, and this is what the section is for:**
CliftonStrengths is closer to how he actually operates. So the Driver result is
the one to be suspicious of.

The honest explanation, offered as suspicion rather than certainty: on a
self-report instrument transparent enough that every item telegraphs which end is
the assertive one, he answered as the person he wanted to be. Fast to decide.
Takes risks. Carefree. Those are aspirational for a Deliberative-first person.
Nobody sits down intending to inflate a self-assessment. It happens because
answering honestly requires already knowing yourself in the exact way the
instrument claims to measure, and anyone who had that would not need the
instrument.

Hedges must stay in, without letting the section hide behind them. Two years
passed and people change. The instruments may measure different layers, one
asking how you come across and the other where instinct runs, so it is genuinely
possible that observers see a Driver because the deliberation is fast and
internal. The section acknowledges these and still lands on the simplest reading.

**Why this closes the essay.** Section 6 argued that people do not have an easy
time understanding what they are doing, that they just *do*, and that
introspection is unreliable and mandatory. This is that argument arriving as
evidence instead of assertion, and it costs the author something to say, which is
what makes it worth reading. A person sat in a room built for self-knowledge,
answered a questionnaire designed to hand it to him, and got himself wrong.

**Constraint, per direct instruction:** Social Styles appears *only* as
self-reflection. It is never used as a claim about the author, never presented as
a second valid profile, and Driver is never treated as a real description of him.
Its entire function is to be the thing he got wrong.

### Section 8 — Close

Short. No summary of the sections. Return to the first step, now carrying
everything the essay has attached to it: that it is toward something built, that
it is watched, that it teaches by being watched, that it may fail, and that
failing does not release you from the people who moved because you did.

Do not end on the assessments. They are the second-to-last movement, not the
last word. The essay closes on the step.

## 4. Scope Boundary

Deliberately **not** in this post:

- Management technique, frameworks, or anything actionable-in-the-workplace.
- A full CliftonStrengths explainer. The reader gets the five themes and only
  the interpretation the argument needs.
- The author's full 34 ranking. Top five only.
- Any claim that CliftonStrengths is predictive, scientifically settled, or
  necessary. It is used as a description, nothing more.
- A full Social Styles explainer, its questionnaire, its scoring, or the other
  three quadrants beyond what one sentence needs. The instrument's weakness is
  noted briefly and the essay moves on.
- Any defence or takedown of personality assessment as a field. The post is not
  about instruments.
- Plot summary, character introductions, or anything resembling criticism of
  *Ratatouille* or *Andor*. Both are mentioned, not reviewed. Every argument must
  survive a reader who has seen neither, which is the test for whether a
  reference has overstayed.
- Sacrifice written as a virtue in itself, or any line that reads as admiring
  self-destruction. The essay's position is that the ceiling exists and crossing
  it makes you worse at the thing you sacrificed for.
- Any resolution of the intentions problem in Section 5. It stays open.
- Any real workplace anecdote, employer, colleague, or client.

## 5. Anonymization Rules

The site is anonymous and this post is more personal than anything currently on
it, so the rules are tighter rather than looser.

- No employer, client, organization, team, or colleague named or made
  identifiable by description.
- No job titles or industries specific enough to identify where the author
  worked. "A leadership training a few years ago" is the maximum specificity
  permitted about the origin of the assessment.
- The parents-and-children examples in Section 5 stay general. They are
  illustrations of a pattern, not testimony about identifiable people, and must
  not be written in a way that reads as a specific family.
- The crisis-leader example is hypothetical and must read as hypothetical. It is
  not an incident.
- CliftonStrengths and Social Styles results are the author's own and are the
  genuinely personal disclosures in the piece. That is intentional and permitted.
- Both assessments came through employer-run training. Neither the employer nor
  the training provider is named or made identifiable. The Social Styles
  questionnaire is not reproduced, and no provider branding or document footer
  from the handout appears anywhere in the post.
- The two-year gap between the assessments is stated relatively, never as
  calendar dates, since dates plus training programs narrow employment history.

- **Sentence case** for the title and every heading.
- **No rule-of-three grouping.** This essay is unusually exposed to it because it

## 7. File Scope

### Visual treatment

**Amended during implementation.** The original decision below was no visuals at
all. Two inline SVG diagrams were added afterward, on the reasoning that they are
content rather than decoration: they carry structure the prose has to walk
through sequentially and that a reader cannot check for themselves otherwise.

- **Four brakes and one accelerator**, in Section 7. Four themes pulling toward
  caution, Activator alone pulling toward action. The imbalance is the argument.
- **The Social Styles grid**, in Section 7b. Two axes, four quadrants, both
  results marked. Without it, the reader has to take the incompatibility on
  faith.

Hand-written inline SVG in the ink language, no library and no build step,
consuming the `post.css` theme tokens so both diagrams survive the light theme.
A `--post-ink`, `--post-panel`, `--post-panel-text`, and `--post-on-accent` token
were added to both theme blocks in `post.css` to support this.

**Accuracy note discovered while building the grid.** Driver and Analytical are
*not* diagonally opposite in Social Styles. They share the controlled
(low-responsiveness) axis and differ only on assertiveness, so they sit
vertically adjacent. Amiable is Driver's true diagonal opposite. An early draft
of the prose claimed "the box directly opposite Driver" and was corrected. Any
future edit to this passage must preserve that.

**No photographic or illustrated images.** Decided deliberately, not by omission.

The essay's spine is taking a step, so any illustration worth having would show
that pose, and it does not exist. Everything in `public/img/` is a finished flat
PNG of Blueman forward-facing, faces and one-off gag pieces. New poses cannot be
composed out of them, only drawn from scratch. A posable Blueman component set is
on the backlog in `docs/post-workflow.md` and is not a prerequisite for shipping
this post.

Film stills from *Andor* and *Ratatouille* were considered and dropped. The
essay's own constraint is that both references must survive a reader who has seen
neither, and a still pulls attention toward the scene, which works against
references already trimmed to a few sentences each.

**What carries the visual rhythm instead: typographic pull-quotes**, in the ink
language established on the Work page. 2px black outline, flat fill, hard offset
shadow rather than soft blur, olive rule. Two of them, no more:

- Ego's line, in Section 1.
- Luthen's line, in Section 4.

These are the two places the essay quotes someone else, so the treatment marks
borrowed voice rather than decorating at random. Styled in the page's scoped
`<style>` block, no new stylesheet, and they must render legibly in dark mode
since `post.css` has no light variants anyway.

If art happens later, it is a separate pass after the prose exists, when the
essay's actual needs are known instead of guessed.
